> **Ticket:** [#119](https://github.com/Dfscashline21/sideline/issues/119) ·
> **Mode:** hand · **Time:** 3 to 4 hours over two or three sessions ·
> **Needs:** a running pipeline from M5/M6, so the examples feel concrete

## Why this matters

"How do you know your data is right?" comes up in nearly every data
engineering interview, and weak answers list tools ("we use Great
Expectations"). Strong answers use a **vocabulary**: which dimension of
quality a check covers, who promised what to whom (a contract), and how
reliable the data needs to be (an SLO). This lab gives you that
vocabulary, applied to Sideline.

## What you'll produce

- `docs/notes/m9-study.md`: your own one-page notes with the sections
  below, every concept illustrated with a **Sideline** example.
- The ability to define SLI, SLO and SLA out loud and tell them apart
  (the acceptance criterion).

## Session 1 · Quality dimensions (60 min)

**Read:**
- [Google SRE book, "Service Level Objectives"](https://sre.google/sre-book/service-level-objectives/)
  (read this one in session 3 if you prefer; it's the source for SLIs)
- Any introduction to the "data observability pillars": freshness,
  volume, schema, distribution, lineage. Barr Moses' original Monte Carlo
  post is the usual reference.

Four dimensions the ticket names, plus the classic ones you'll hear in
interviews:

| Dimension | Question it answers | A Sideline check |
|---|---|---|
| **Freshness** | Is the data recent enough? | Yesterday's games are in `fact_team_game` by 6 AM PT |
| **Volume** | Is the amount of data plausible? | A game has 300 to 600 play-by-play events, not 104 |
| **Schema** | Are the fields and types what we expect? | `nba_api` still returns `PLAYER_ID`, as an integer |
| **Distribution** | Are the values plausible? | Shot coordinates inside the court; scores non-negative |
| Completeness | Is anything missing? | Every scheduled game has a box score |
| Uniqueness | Is anything duplicated? | One row per `(game_id, team_id)` |
| Consistency | Do related facts agree? | Player points sum to the team score |
| Accuracy | Does it match reality? | Score matches the official result |

## Session 2 · Data contracts (60 min)

**Read:**
- [Open Data Contract Standard](https://bitol-io.github.io/open-data-contract-standard/latest/):
  the "Fundamentals", "Schema", "Data quality" and "Service-level
  agreement" sections.
- The [`datacontract-cli` README](https://github.com/datacontract/datacontract-cli):
  skim the command list (`lint`, `test`, `breaking`, `changelog`).

A **data contract** is an agreement between a data producer and its
consumers, written as a file that a machine can check:

- **Schema:** fields, types, required or not, allowed values.
- **Quality:** rules the data must meet.
- **Ownership:** who to call when it breaks.
- **Service levels:** how fresh, how complete, how often.

The key idea: a contract turns "please don't break my dashboard" into a
**CI check**. Changing the contract in a way that breaks a guarantee
(removing a field, narrowing a type, tightening an SLA) fails the build,
so the change becomes a conversation instead of an incident.

### Exercise 2.1: breaking or not?

For each change to a contract, decide whether it's **breaking** for
consumers, and why:

1. Add an optional field `arena_name`.
2. Remove the field `PLUS_MINUS`.
3. Change `GAME_DATE` from `string` to `date`.
4. Make the optional field `MIN` required.
5. Widen `PTS` from "0 to 100" to "0 to 120".
6. Change freshness from "by 6 AM" to "by 9 AM".

<details>
<summary>Discussion</summary>

1. Not breaking: consumers ignore fields they don't know.
2. Breaking: anything reading it fails.
3. Breaking in practice: code parsing strings may fail; arguably a fix.
   Treat type changes as breaking.
4. Depends whose side you're on. For the **producer** it's a new promise
   (fine). For consumers it's stricter data (fine). It's breaking for
   whoever *produces* the data if they can't meet it.
5. Widening a range consumers relied on can break their assumptions
   (a chart axis, a check). Ranges are subtle; `datacontract breaking`
   has its own rules. Note which way it reports this in M9.2.
6. Breaking: a weaker service level is a broken promise.

</details>

## Session 3 · SLIs, SLOs and SLAs (60 min)

**Read:** the Google SRE chapter above. Pay attention to error budgets.

| Term | What it is | Sideline example |
|---|---|---|
| **SLI** (indicator) | A measurement | Time when yesterday's last box score landed in gold |
| **SLO** (objective) | A target for the SLI, over a window | By 6:00 AM PT on 99% of game days, over 90 days |
| **SLA** (agreement) | A promise to someone else, with consequences | "If we miss it more than 3 days a quarter, the client gets a refund" |
| **Error budget** | 100% − SLO: how much failure you're allowed | 1% of 90 game days = 0.9 days, so not even one miss per window |

The relationship to memorise: **SLI is measured, SLO is the internal
target for that measurement, SLA is the external promise.** The SLA is
always looser than the SLO, so you notice and fix problems before you
owe anyone anything.

### Exercise 3.1

### Exercise 3.2: error-budget arithmetic

The M9.6 target is "99% of days". If you measure daily over a 30-day
window, how many misses does 99% allow? Over 90 days? Over a year? What
does that tell you about choosing a window for a daily SLO?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

30 days × 1% = 0.3, so **zero** misses allowed: one bad day breaks the
month. 90 days → 0.9 → still zero. 365 days → 3.65 → three misses.
With daily measurements, a 99% target only makes sense over long
windows; otherwise say "99%" but mean "100%". Bring this to M9.6.

</details>

## Session 4 · Alerting without alert fatigue (30 min)

This is the module's ready-when question, so start thinking now.

**Read:** the "Monitoring Distributed Systems" chapter of the SRE book
(the section on symptoms vs causes).

Principles to put in your notes, in your own words:

- **Alert on symptoms users feel** (gold data late, wrong numbers
  published), not on every cause (one retry failed).
- **Severity decides the channel.** Page for "consumers are affected
  now". Ticket or daily digest for "this will matter soon". Log the rest.
- **Every alert must be actionable** and link to a runbook (M6.6).
- **Tune thresholds from data** (M9.5), and track how often each alert
  fires and how often it was real.
