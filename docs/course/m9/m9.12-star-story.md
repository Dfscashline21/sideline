# M9.12 · STAR story from your current job

> **Ticket:** [#130](https://github.com/Dfscashline21/sideline/issues/130) ·
> **Mode:** hand · **Time:** 45 min ·
> **Prompt:** *A bad-data incident at work: how it was found, how long
> it took, and the checks that would have caught it.*

The STAR format, word budgets and common mistakes are in the
[M0.9 lab](../m0/m0.9-star-story.md). Reread its table before starting.
This lab covers only what's specific to a data-quality story.

## Finding the story

Questions to jog memory:

- **Found by a consumer.** A stakeholder or customer spotted wrong
  numbers before the data team did. How long had it been wrong?
- **Silent failures.** A job that "succeeded" but loaded zero rows, half
  a file, or yesterday's file again.
- **Upstream changes.** A source system renamed a column, changed a
  type or units, or started sending NULLs.
- **Duplicates or late corrections.** Revenue double-counted after a
  re-run; a correction in the source that never reached the report.

The prompt has three parts. Make sure the story covers all three:

1. **How it was found:** by a check, a person, or luck?
2. **How long it took:** to notice, to diagnose, to fix. This is where
   your number usually comes from.
3. **Which checks would have caught it:** name them in M9's vocabulary
   (a contract on the source, a volume check, a freshness SLO, a
   reconciliation check). This is where you show what you learned.

## What makes a data-quality story strong

- **Time to detect** as the headline number. "Wrong for 11 days before
  finance noticed" is memorable; the fix you added that would have caught
  it in one day is the punchline.
- **Blameless framing.** Describe the missing safeguard, not who made the
  mistake (see M9.9).
- **A check you actually added,** or proposed, with the result: incidents
  caught since, or time to detect after.

If you didn't fix it at the time, the Action can be what you'd do now,
**said honestly**: "Looking back, a volume check on daily rows would have
caught it on day one; here's how I'd set the threshold." Interviewers
respect reflection; they don't respect invented heroics.

## Follow-ups to prepare

Write a one-line answer to each:

1. "How would you have set the threshold for that check without causing
   false alarms?"
2. "Who should have been alerted, and how?"
3. "How do you make sure it can't happen again?" (prevent vs detect,
   from M9.9)

## Where to save it

Private notes alongside your other STAR stories, **never** this repo.
Close the issue with "Saved to private notes" and no details.

## Done when

- [ ] S, T, A, R in under 200 words, covering how it was found, how long it took, and the checks that would have caught it
- [ ] Includes a number (time to detect, rows affected, cost or people)
- [ ] Saved to private notes, not the public repo
- [ ] Three follow-up answers drafted
