# M12.8 · Blog post: point-in-time joins

> **Ticket:** [#159](https://github.com/Dfscashline21/sideline/issues/159) ·
> **Mode:** hand · **Time:** 4 to 5 hours ·
> **Needs:** M12.1 to M12.4 (you'll use their numbers)

## Why this matters

Writing it down is how you find out whether you understand it. A post
that explains leakage to a stranger, with real numbers from your own
data, is also the most reusable portfolio piece in this module: it's the
answer to the M12 ready-when question, written once, linkable from your
resume.

It's `mode:hand`: write it yourself. An agent may proofread at the end
(step 6), not draft.

## What you'll produce

- `docs/blog/point-in-time-joins.md` (and wherever you publish posts:
  your site, dev.to, Medium, LinkedIn)
- A link from the README (the acceptance criterion: published **and**
  linked)

## Steps

### 1. Pick the reader and the one idea (15 min)

Write one sentence: *"After reading this, a data engineer who's never
trained a model will understand that ___."* Everything in the post
serves that sentence. Suggested reader: a mid-level data engineer who
builds tables that ML teams use.

### 2. Outline (30 min)

A structure that works for technical posts:

1. **Hook (one paragraph).** The opening-night example from M12.1: the
   naive join already "knows" Boston will finish 64-18 before the first
   game. Concrete beats abstract.
2. **The numbers.** Your M12.1 table: always-home, point-in-time,
   naive. The naive model *looks* best, and that's the problem.
3. **Why it happens.** Prediction time; "known at tip-off"; one small
   diagram of a timeline with a game and the data on either side.
4. **The fix.** The `ASOF JOIN` with strictly-before, and the frame that
   excludes the current game. Short SQL, only the lines that matter.
5. **The subtle ones.** Two of: time zones (M12.2 injury report),
   `LEAD` vs `LAG`, fitting a scaler on all data (M12.4), random splits.
6. **How to stop it coming back.** The truncation test from M12.3, in a
   few sentences and a short snippet.
7. **Takeaway.** One paragraph, plus a link to the repo.

### 3. Draft (2 hours)

Rules that keep technical posts readable:

- Every claim with a number uses **your** measured numbers, with the
  data and season range stated. Re-run the M12.1 query on the day you
  publish and use what it returns.
- SQL snippets under ~15 lines. Link to the full file in the repo.
- One diagram. A timeline (games as dots, tip-off as a vertical line,
  "allowed" on the left, "leak" on the right) explains more than a
  paragraph.
- Define each term once, the first time it appears: prediction time,
  as-of join, leakage.

### 4. Check every number and snippet (30 min)

- Re-run every SQL snippet as written in the post. Copy-paste bugs in
  published SQL are common and embarrassing.
- Check every number against today's output.
- Check every link (repo files, docs).

✅ **Checkpoint:** a reader could reproduce your headline table from the
post and the repo alone.

### 5. Get one human reader (30 min)

Ask someone (a colleague, a friend in tech) to read it and tell you the
one idea in their own words. If it doesn't match your step-1 sentence,
the post needs work, not the reader.

### 6. Proofread, publish, link

An agent may now check spelling, grammar and clarity. Accept fixes, not
rewrites. Then:

- Publish to your chosen platform, and keep the Markdown in `docs/blog/`.
- Add the link to the README (the "decisions" or a "writing" section).
- Optional: post a short summary with the headline table on LinkedIn
  (PF.7 will update your profile anyway).

## Gotchas

- **Stale numbers.** Source data gets re-published; write the date and
  season range next to every number.
- **Overclaiming.** "Leakage inflated accuracy by 4.5 points on NBA games
  2021-22 to 2025-26 with a one-feature rule" is defensible. "Leakage
  ruins ML" isn't.
- **Copying the course text.** Write it in your own words. The course is
  scaffolding; the post is yours.
