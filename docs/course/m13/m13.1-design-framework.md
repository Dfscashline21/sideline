# M13.1 · Study: a design framework

> **Ticket:** [#162](https://github.com/Dfscashline21/sideline/issues/162) ·
> **Mode:** hand · **Time:** 3 to 4 hours ·
> **Needs:** M0 to M12 (or as far as you've got); your ADRs

## Why this matters

An open question like "design a player-tracking pipeline" is designed to
feel too big. Candidates who freeze, or jump straight to "Kafka into
Spark into Snowflake", fail. Candidates who calmly walk a checklist —
requirements first, then data flow, then each layer, then how it breaks —
pass, even with an imperfect design.

The ticket lists the checklist's ten areas: **requirements, data flow,
storage, compute, serving, quality, security, cost, failure modes,
evolution.** Your job is to turn that list into *your* one-page tool:
questions you'll ask, numbers you'll estimate, and a time budget for a
45-minute interview.

## What you'll produce

- `docs/notes/m13-framework.md`: **one page**, in your own words.
- `docs/design/template.md`: the doc template you'll use for M13.2 to M13.4.

## Steps

### 1. Mine your own system first (45 min)

Before reading anyone else's framework, list the decisions you already
made building Sideline, and map each to one of the ten areas:

| Area | A Sideline decision | Where it's recorded |
|---|---|---|
| Requirements | e.g. NBA daily freshness SLO | M9.6 |
| Storage | Iceberg over Delta | ADR-003 |
| Compute | DuckDB vs Spark crossover | M7.9 |
| … | | |

✅ **Checkpoint:** at least one real decision in eight of the ten areas.
The gaps show you which areas you'll need to practise most.

### 2. Write the question bank (60 min)

For each area, write two or three questions you'd ask in an interview.
Make them specific enough to change the design.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1 · Nudge: what's a good requirements question?</summary>

Weak: "How much data is there?"

Strong: "How many events per second at peak, and is peak 10x the average
(live games) or flat?" The answer picks between batch and streaming.

Cover **functional** (what it does, who uses it) and **non-functional**
(latency, freshness, volume, retention, availability, compliance).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2 · Approach: areas people forget</summary>

Quality, security, cost and evolution get skipped under time pressure,
and they're where senior candidates stand out. For each, have one
ready-made question:

- **Quality:** "What's the cost of a wrong number vs a late number?"
- **Security:** "Is there PII or regulated data, and who may see it?"
- **Cost:** "What's the budget order of magnitude: $100s or $100k a month?"
- **Evolution:** "What's the likely 10x change in two years: more leagues,
  more users, lower latency?"

</details>

### 3. Back-of-envelope estimates (45 min)

Most design answers turn on one or two numbers. Practise the arithmetic
until it's quick. Write down these reference values yourself (look them
up and check them, don't copy):

- seconds in a day (≈ 86,400 → "≈ 10⁵")
- bytes for a typical event row (100 bytes to 1 KB)
- rough throughput of one machine for a sequential disk scan, and of one
  Kafka/Redpanda partition
- your own measured numbers: rows/sec of your M2 extractor, M7 Spark job
  times, M11 p95 latency

Then do one estimate end to end on paper:

> One NBA season of play-by-play: games × events per game × bytes per
> event. Use your M4 Iceberg table to check the real size.

✅ **Checkpoint:** your estimate is within 10x of the real table size.
Within 10x is good enough to choose an architecture. Write down which
assumption was furthest out.

### 4. The 45-minute time budget (20 min)

Decide how you'll spend an interview. A common split:

| Minutes | Phase |
|---|---|
| 0–8 | Requirements and estimates (ask, then **summarise back**) |
| 8–15 | High-level data flow diagram: sources → storage → compute → serving |
| 15–30 | Deep dive on the 1–2 hardest parts (the interviewer often picks) |
| 30–38 | Quality, failure modes, security, cost |
| 38–45 | Evolution ("at 10x…") and questions |

Adjust it, but have one. The commonest failure is spending 25 minutes on
requirements.

### 5. Write the one page (30 min)

Close everything. From memory, write `docs/notes/m13-framework.md`:
the ten areas, each with your best one or two questions, the estimate
cheat sheet, and the time budget. If it's longer than one page, cut.

### 6. Write the doc template (20 min)

`docs/design/template.md`, used for M13.2 to M13.4. Suggested sections:

```markdown
# <Title>
Status: draft · Author: · Date:

## 1. Context and problem       (why now, who's affected)
## 2. Requirements              (functional; non-functional with numbers)
## 3. Estimates                 (volume, throughput, storage, cost)
## 4. Proposed design           (diagram + walk through the data flow)
## 5. Key decisions and trade-offs   (option A vs B, why A, what we give up)
## 6. Quality, security, failure modes
## 7. Cost
## 8. Evolution and open questions
## 9. Alternatives considered
```

## Gotchas

- **Copying someone's framework verbatim.** You won't recall it under
  pressure. Your wording, your order.
- **Skipping "summarise back".** Repeating the requirements in one
  sentence before designing catches misunderstandings and shows control.
- **Over-precise estimates.** Round to one significant figure. The
  interviewer wants the order of magnitude and your method.
