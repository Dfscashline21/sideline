# M13.3 · Design doc: player-tracking pipeline

> **Ticket:** [#164](https://github.com/Dfscashline21/sideline/issues/164) ·
> **Mode:** hand · **Time:** 4 to 6 hours ·
> **Needs:** M13.1, M4 (Iceberg, partitioning, compaction), M7 (Spark)

## Why this matters

Optical tracking records the x/y (and for the ball, z) position of every
player and the ball many times a second. The ticket says **25 Hz, every
player, every game**. That's a different shape from anything you've
built: play-by-play is hundreds of events per game; tracking is
*millions* of rows per game.

This prompt tests whether you can **size** a problem before designing it,
and whether you know how storage layout (partitioning, file sizes,
sort order) decides what's cheap to query. That's exactly what M4 taught.

## What you'll produce

`docs/design/002-player-tracking-pipeline.md`: 2 to 4 pages, your
template, a diagram, and a trade-offs section.

## Steps

### 1. Requirements (40 min)

Assume a league-owned system (or a team's analytics department). Write
and answer, as labelled assumptions:

- **Consumers:** analysts querying historical games? Coaches watching
  near-live? Broadcast graphics? ML training (M12)? Each changes latency
  and query shape.
- **Latency:** is "near-live" needed at all, or is "available 1 hour after
  the final buzzer" enough? Don't assume streaming.
- **Query patterns:** "all positions for one player across a season",
  "all 10 players during one possession", "every shot's defender
  distance". Write five real ones. They decide the layout.
- **History:** how many seasons back? Is data ever corrected
  (re-processed camera calibration)?

✅ **Checkpoint:** five concrete queries written as plain sentences, each
with a target response time.

### 2. Size it yourself (45 min)

Do this on paper before reading the hint.

- objects tracked at once (players on court + ball)
- samples per second (25)
- seconds recorded per game (game clock only, or real elapsed time
  including stoppages? Choose, and say why)
- bytes per sample (IDs, timestamp, x, y, z, maybe velocity)
- games per season (all leagues you include)

Compute: rows per game, rows per season, raw bytes per season, and the
Parquet-compressed size (assume a compression ratio and say what it is).

<details>
<summary>Hint · Check your arithmetic shape</summary>

Rows per game ≈ objects × 25 × seconds recorded. With roughly 11 objects
and ~2,900 seconds of game clock, that's on the order of **800,000 rows
per game**, and roughly a billion per NBA regular season. If you counted
stoppage time, it's a few times more. Your exact number depends on your
choices. What matters is the order of magnitude and that you can derive it.

Then ask: does one season fit on one laptop's disk compressed? Does a
query over one season fit in DuckDB, or is this Spark territory? Use
your M7.9 crossover benchmark to argue it.

</details>

### 3. Storage layout (60 min)

This is the deep dive an interviewer will push on. Decide and justify:

- **Table format** (Iceberg, per ADR-003) and **partitioning**: by game
  date? by game? by team? Remember M4.4 (partition evolution) and why
  over-partitioning creates millions of tiny files.
- **Sort order / clustering** inside files, so "one player in one game"
  reads few row groups (M4.1: column stats and file skipping).
- **File size targets** and a **compaction** plan (M4.7).
- **Derived tables:** do you precompute possession-level or
  shot-level aggregates (like M1.7's summary table) so most queries never
  touch raw samples?

<details>
<summary>Hint · Partition by what the queries filter on</summary>

List your five queries against each candidate partition key. A key that
helps one query and forces full scans on three is a bad key. Often the
answer is a coarse partition (e.g. by date or game) plus a sort on
player and timestamp, plus summary tables for the common questions.

</details>

### 4. Ingestion and processing (40 min)

- Data arrives how: a file per game after it ends, or a stream during it?
  Draw both and pick one for v1. Justify with your latency requirement.
- Where do validation checks go (M9)? E.g. players out of court bounds,
  sample gaps, more than 5 players per team on court.
- How are corrections handled: overwrite a game's partition, or new
  snapshot plus time travel?

### 5. Trade-offs, failure modes, cost (45 min)

At least three trade-offs. Candidates:

| Decision | Options |
|---|---|
| Batch per game vs streaming | Simplicity and cost vs freshness |
| Raw sample retention | Keep forever vs downsample old seasons (e.g. 25 Hz → 5 Hz) |
| Query engine | DuckDB on a big box vs Spark vs a warehouse (M10.5) |
| Precomputed aggregates | Faster common queries vs pipeline complexity and staleness |

Failure modes: a camera feed drops for 3 minutes; a game's file is
duplicated; calibration is wrong for an arena for a month. How is each
detected, and what's the fix?

Cost: storage per season at your compressed size and your cloud's
object-storage price (look up the current price); compute for a typical
day's ingestion.

## Gotchas

- **Defaulting to Kafka.** If nobody needs it live, a file per game is
  simpler, cheaper and easier to reprocess. Streaming must be earned by a
  requirement.
- **Partitioning by player.** Hundreds of players × hundreds of dates
  gives a small-files problem. Check your file counts.
- **Showing arithmetic only in your head.** Put it in the doc; reviewers
  check your method.
