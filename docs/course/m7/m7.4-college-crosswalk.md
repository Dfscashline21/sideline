# M7.4 · College staging, dims and identity crosswalk build

> **Ticket:** [#97](https://github.com/Dfscashline21/sideline/issues/97) ·
> **Mode:** hand · **Time:** 5 to 6 hours ·
> **Needs:** M3.4 (crosswalk design), M5 (dbt project, `dim_player`),
> M7.3 (college raw data landed)

## Why this matters

"Is this the same person?" is **entity resolution**, and it's in every
data platform: customers across CRMs, products across suppliers, players
across leagues. Interviewers want to hear three things: you match on the
strongest evidence first, you measure precision against known answers,
and **you never auto-merge when unsure**. Ambiguous cases go to a review
queue a human can clear.

## What you'll produce

- Staging models for `ncaa_m` and `ncaa_w` (`stg_ncaa_m__player_box`,
  `stg_ncaa_m__pbp`, …) following your NBA/WNBA staging conventions.
- College rows flowing into `dim_player`, `dim_team`, `dim_game`.
- `player_crosswalk` populated for college ↔ pro links, using the shape
  from M3.4: `player_crosswalk(player_id, source, source_player_id,
  match_method, match_confidence, …)`.
- `player_crosswalk_review`: ambiguous candidates, never auto-linked.
- A test set of known college-to-pro players that must link correctly.

## Background: what the data actually looks like

Checked against the ESPN files (NBA 2022 to 2026, men's college 2008 to 2026):

- **Shared IDs for recent players.** ESPN often uses the same
  `athlete_id` in college and the NBA: Zion Williamson (4395628, Duke),
  Ja Morant (4279888, Murray State), Paolo Banchero, Cooper Flagg. About
  700 of ~1,050 NBA players in those seasons appear in the college data
  under the same ID.
- **Different IDs for older players.** Stephen Curry is 32284 at
  Davidson and 3975 in the NBA; Marcus Smart 61669 at Oklahoma State and
  2990992 in the NBA.
- **Name collisions.** "Tyler Johnson" in the NBA (first NBA season 2022)
  and "Tyler Johnson" at Virginia Tech (last college season 2026) are
  different people. Names alone will create false links.
- **Other sources have their own IDs.** Your NBA extractor (M2) uses
  `nba_api` person IDs, which are not ESPN IDs. CollegeBasketballData.com
  has its own too. That's why the crosswalk has `source` and
  `source_player_id`.

## Steps

### 1. Staging models (90 min)

Build college staging the same way you built WNBA staging (M6.3):
rename, cast, one model per source entity, no business logic. Two
college-specific rules:

- Drop the ESPN **team-total rows** (`athlete_display_name` like
  `- Team`, `-`, `Team`). Write a test that none survive.
- Keep `athlete_id` exactly as the source gives it, as
  `source_player_id` with `source = 'espn'`.

✅ **Checkpoint:** `dbt build --select staging.ncaa_m staging.ncaa_w`
passes, including your no-team-rows test.

### 2. Build the known-answer test set first (45 min)

Before writing matching logic, write down what "correct" means. Create
`transform/seeds/crosswalk_known_links.csv` with 15 to 25 players you can
verify from public records (draft pages, the CBBD draft picks
endpoint, Basketball Reference):

```csv
player_name,college_source,college_source_player_id,pro_source,pro_source_player_id,note
Stephen Curry,espn,32284,espn,3975,different ESPN IDs
Zion Williamson,espn,4395628,espn,4395628,same ESPN ID
```

Include on purpose:

- several **shared-ID** players and several **different-ID** players
- at least one **name collision** pair that must **not** link
- women's players: a few WNBA ↔ `ncaa_w` links. Check yourself whether
  ESPN shares IDs for women too; don't assume
- a player who **transferred** between colleges (one person, two schools)

Spending CBBD quota on draft picks is the use your M7.2 design probably
reserved budget for: one call per draft year gives you ground-truth links.

### 3. Design the matching tiers (30 min)

Write the tiers down before coding. Strongest evidence first; each
player is resolved by the first tier that fires.

<details>
<summary>Hint 1 · Nudge</summary>

What evidence do you have, from strongest to weakest? Identical source
IDs within the same provider. An external authority (draft records).
Name plus a plausible career timeline. Name alone.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2 · Approach: three tiers and a queue</summary>

| Tier | Rule | `match_method` | `match_confidence` | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Same `source` and same `source_player_id` in college and pro data | `same_source_id` | 1.0 | Link |
| 2 | Draft record links the college player to the pro player | `draft_record` | 0.95 | Link |
| 3 | Normalised name matches **and** the timeline fits (last college season ≤ first pro season ≤ last college season + 2) **and** exactly one candidate | `name_timeline` | e.g. 0.8 | Link |
| – | Name matches but the timeline doesn't fit, or **more than one** candidate fits | – | – | `player_crosswalk_review` |

Name normalisation: lowercase, strip punctuation and suffixes (`Jr.`,
`II`, `III`), collapse spaces. Write it once, as a macro.

</details>

### 4. Build it in dbt (90 min)

Suggested models:

- `int_player_candidates`: every (college player, pro player) pair that
  shares a normalised name, with both timelines.
- `int_player_matches`: candidates scored by tier; count candidates per
  college player and per pro player.
- `player_crosswalk`: tier 1 to 3 winners, plus every source ID for
  unmatched players mapped to their own new `player_id`.
- `player_crosswalk_review`: the ambiguous ones, with the reason
  (`multiple_candidates`, `timeline_mismatch`).

<details>
<summary>Hint 3 · Skeleton: the "exactly one candidate" rule</summary>

```sql
with scored as (
    select *,
        count(*) over (partition by college_source_player_id) as n_pro_candidates,
        count(*) over (partition by pro_source_player_id)     as n_college_candidates
    from {{ ref('int_player_candidates') }}
    where timeline_fits
)
select * from scored
where n_pro_candidates = 1 and n_college_candidates = 1     -- tier 3 link
-- everything else with a name match goes to review
```

Counting in **both** directions matters: two college "Tyler Johnson"s
fitting one pro is just as ambiguous as the reverse.

</details>

Then make sure `dim_player` uses the crosswalk: a player who played in
college and the NBA has **one durable `player_id`**, with SCD2 rows
(`valid_from`, `valid_to`, `is_current`, `player_sk`) covering both
careers.

### 5. Test against the known answers (45 min)

dbt tests that read the seed:

- every known link resolves to the **same** `player_id` on both sides
- every known "must not link" pair resolves to **different** `player_id`s
- `player_crosswalk` is unique on `(source, source_player_id)`
- nothing in `player_crosswalk_review` also appears in `player_crosswalk`

✅ **Checkpoint:** all known links pass. Then look at the review table:
are its cases genuinely ambiguous? Clear five by hand (add them to the
seed) and note how long it took.

### 6. Measure and record (20 min)

In `docs/notes/m7.md`: how many links per tier, how many in review,
results on the known set. One sentence on what precision you'd expect
from tier 3 and why.

## Gotchas

- **Unique keys break silently.** A player matched by tier 1 *and* tier 3
  to different people produces two crosswalk rows for one source ID.
  The uniqueness test catches it; run it.
- **Transfers aren't duplicates.** One college player at two schools has
  one ESPN ID; don't split them by team.
- **Rebuilding changes `player_id`s** if you generate them from row
  order. Derive durable IDs deterministically (e.g. from the first
  source ID seen), or downstream facts will re-key on every run.
